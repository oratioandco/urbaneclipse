Sketch 1 · the minimal fix

Line only

What the strip draws today, minus the area fill that read as a second series. Rain joins as teal columns rising from the base of the sky area — taller, more likely.

GoodSmallest change; keeps one visual voice for weather (one line + rain).
CostCover is still an abstraction you must decode; rain columns can crowd the line at busy hours.
Sketch 2

Sky wash

No line at all: the band is the sky. The phase gradient shows through a grey veil whose opacity is the cloud cover — clear hours glow, soup goes flat grey. Rain as falling teal streaks from the top.

GoodMatches how the scene itself looks, and how photographers think: "is there a sky or not". Reads at phone size.
CostPrecision is gone — 40% vs 60% is feel, not a number (the hover keeps the number).
Sketch 3

Three decks

The app already fetches low, mid and high cloud separately — this shows them. Three stacked strips, darkest nearest the ground. Low cloud is what kills a shot; high cirrus can make a sunset. Rain hangs under the low deck.

GoodExplains why an hour fails, and shows the good-sunset case (high cloud over, low clear) that total cover hides.
CostThree encodings in 24 px of sky — busy at this size; needs a legend the first time.
Sketch 4

Hour bars

One bar per hour, its height the cover, rounded at the top. A raining hour wears a teal cap as tall as the chance of rain. Nothing subtle about it.

GoodStrongest pure magnitude read; gaps between bars keep hours countable.
CostThe bars take over the band — the day stops feeling like a sky and starts feeling like a spreadsheet; golden-hour nuance is flattened into hour bins.
Sketch 5

Verdict ribbon

Skips cloud entirely and draws the answer: each hour graded the way the cards grade shots — green good, amber fair, red-brown poor — as one smooth ribbon over the light pill. Rain hatches the ribbon it touches.

GoodZero decoding: the ribbon is the decision. Speaks the same colour language as the cards' grade dots.
CostStatus colours spend fast; a full-red Saturday feels alarmist for weather. Thresholds become policy, visible in the drawing.
Sketch 6

Photo windows

The inverse question: instead of drawing weather, light up the hours worth shooting. Shootable hours keep the full phase colour; the rest fade to grey. Rain streaks only live in the grey.

GoodAnswers "when do I go" in one glance; beautiful on a covered week — the page simply stays dark.
CostHides the why (was it cloud or rain?); near-miss hours look identical to hopeless ones. Best paired with hover detail.
Sketch 7

Dots and spread

An instrument, not a picture: per hour, a dot at the mid-cloud reading with a hairline spanning low to high deck. Rain as teal drops along the base, sized by probability.

GoodMost honest to the data — shows the deck spread that a single number smooths away.
CostQuiet to the point of shy; the at-a-distance verdict is weak, and 24 dots can read as noise.
Sketch 8

Two skies

One axis, two opposing fills: cloud rises from the base as grey, rain hangs from the top as teal. Where they nearly meet, the hour is write-off; a wide gap between them is shooting light.

GoodRain finally gets equal billing; the "gap" shape gives a felt sense of headroom.
CostTwo fills sharing one axis invites misreading (looks stacked); needs its legend always visible.
Round 2 · the combination, eight ways

Wash + decks + a quiet verdict

Your three picks fused eight genuinely different ways. The ingredients are constant — the sky wash (the band as the sky, the pill's phase ramp continued upward), the three decks (low, mid, high cloud, the low one pressing on the shooting ground), and a verdict far quieter than the round-1 ribbon — but each sketch merges them differently: the decks as the wash's vertical profile, as steps, as columns, as smoky veils, as contours inside one wash, as only the decisive deck, or not drawn at all while the verdict takes over. Rain is settled in every one: a teal line laid on the ground, silent when dry, thicker when actually wet. The day's sun and moon shots stand on the pill. In a winter where direct light is scarce, the strip should be quiet where hours fail and luminous where they work. Hover any band for the hour.

Round 2 · 1

Sky profile

The deepest fusion: no deck drawing at all. Each hour's sky is one gradient whose vertical profile is the deck data — grey at the bottom is low cloud, grey at the top is high. The wash and the decks are literally the same pixels. Verdict: the under-glow.

GoodOne drawing, zero duplication; reads as a real sky with altitude baked in.
CostDeck boundaries blur together — "which deck" needs the hover more than anywhere else.
Round 2 · 2

Stepped strata

The judge panels' converged skeleton, kept as the reference: three crisp grey bands at their altitudes, opacity the cover. Honest hourly steps. Verdict: under-glow for good, pencil scratches through poor.

GoodThe most legible WHY — grey on the pill vs grey at the top is unmistakable.
CostReads most like a chart; three bands can look like decoration on quiet days.
Round 2 · 3

Columns

The decks stacked per hour as three slim blocks — the round-1 hour bars, but telling the truth about altitude. Adjacent columns merge into a city skyline of cloud. Verdict: the lit floor and dashed rule.

GoodHours stay countable; the gap keeps every hour a distinct object you can point at.
CostMost instrument-like, least sky-like; the skyline pun is a distraction.
Round 2 · 4

Soft veils

The decks as feathered sheets of haze — no hard band edges, each fading in and out vertically so mixed decks blend like real atmosphere. Overcast still collapses to flat grey. Verdict: a crown hairline at the top edge over good hours.

GoodThe most atmospheric fusion; matches how a sky actually layers.
CostThe softness that flatters also hides — deck amounts get harder to compare.
Round 2 · 5

Contours in the wash

One total veil, as round-1's sky wash — and the decks only as faint light seams inside it, appearing only where one deck dominates the hour, like contour lines in fog. A quiet sky stays one smooth grey. Verdict: the same crown.

GoodKeeps round-1's clean wash silhouette and still answers WHY, but only when WHY is one deck.
CostSeams are the subtlest weather mark here — easy to miss, easy to misread as banding.
Round 2 · 6

The decisive deck

Radical economy: each hour draws only the deck that decides it — the biggest one, at its altitude. Clear hours draw nothing at all. The day's story is told by its villain. Verdict: lit floor and dashed rule.

GoodHalf the ink of the strata for most of the decision; nothing decorative survives.
CostLoses the mixed-sky texture — two medium decks read as one, nuance is gone.
Round 2 · 7

Brightness only

The verdict with zero added marks: a plain total wash for weather, and the grade spoken only in brightness — good hours' pill lifts a little, poor hours' dims. No green, no red-brown, nothing new to learn. The light itself is the verdict.

GoodImpossible to mistake for a second grading system; survives every vision, every print.
CostThe quietest verdict here is also the weakest — a 10% dim is honest but nearly subliminal.
Round 2 · 8

Lit ticks

The verdict moves onto the shots themselves: a plain total wash for weather, and each sun or moon tick whose hour grades good wears a soft halo. No shot, no verdict — on a covered day the ticks simply stand unlit, which is the whole message.

GoodVerdict exactly where a decision exists — pure scarcity framing, no hour-by-hour wallpaper.
CostOnly grades the few shot moments; the hours between stay ungraded without hover.
Round 3 · the restructure

One light bar, solid status strips, clay icons

Built from your notes: the light-colours bar is the same every day, so it now appears exactly once — a shared axis at the bottom of each card, with the hour marks. Every day keeps the three cloud decks, and gains a solid status strip on its own bottom edge where good, fair and poor are spoken in full-strength colour — a clear opportunity has to stand out, not whisper. Rain falls as clay-style droplets with gloss and shadow, and the chips carry tiny clay suns and rainclouds — standing in for true 3d sprites, which the scene itself can render later. Hover any day: the crosshair follows the whole column and every day reads out that hour.

Round 3 · A

Beacon strip

Full-colour status strip on light ground, gloss on top like everything else in the app. Where a good window runs, the strip casts light up into the clouds — the one place the sky and the verdict touch.

GoodUnmistakable at arm's length; the cast glow points at the window without another mark.
CostLoudest of the four — status colour spends the most ink here.
Round 3 · B

Dark ground

Each day sits on night. The strip emits light only where there is opportunity: a warm gold chip for go, dim amber for maybe, and for no — nothing at all. Scarcity as the design: you are looking for light in the dark, and the strip is where it shows.

GoodThe clearest possible "when do I go" — gold chips on a dark strip; a covered week stays almost black.
CostFair reads as half-good; poor is silent, so "why not" lives in the clouds and hover only.
Round 3 · C

Icon chips

Status colour in rounded chips, and the weather drawn twice as language: a tiny clay sun sits in every good chip, a clay raincloud in every poor-and-wet one. Reads at a glance even in greyscale, because the icons carry the meaning, not the hue.

GoodDelightful and self-explaining — no legend survives past the first look.
CostChips segment the day; a long golden window reads as many chips, not one window.
Round 3 · D

Framed windows

The quietest strip (one thin status line) plus the loudest opportunity mark: every shootable window gets a glowing frame around the whole day — clouds, rain and all — like a ticket for the times that will work.

GoodThe frame is the single loudest "here" signal anywhere in the three rounds; weather stays honest underneath.
CostFrames can crowd when windows sit close; two adjacent frames nearly touch.
Round 4 · built from D, by an Opus 5.5 subsession

Rain that speaks, windows for the moon too

Round 3·D's skeleton — deck strata, quiet status ink, and the loud window frame as the opportunity mark — carried over, with its two gaps fixed. Rain is no longer three big identical drops: each variant draws type and intensity its own way (a fog whose height is mm, a bead census, fronts vs showers, a cumulative wetness trace). Frames wrap the shots, not the daylight: for the sun, only the blue and golden hours around sunrise and sunset earn a ticket — clear midday stays in the status line but frames nothing. The moon gets windows too, and the same logic: a moon rising or setting — low, behind landmarks, in a twilight sky — lit ≥ 25% and clear enough, earns a silver-blue frame (crescent punch, over the navy stretch of the axis); a high 3am moon is scene lighting, not a shot. New moon means no frame at all: Saturday's 22% crescent shows its silver rail (moon up) but earns no window. A rainlocked day like Tomorrow draws empty — not red.

Round 4 · A

Wet ground

Rain becomes a teal fog on each day's horizon: its height is the mm depth, its colour density the probability. A front is one continuous sheet, a shower a lone mound, drizzle a 2.6px damp film — the shape does the typing for free.

GoodInstant "how much water is in the air"; continuity across hours is drawn, not implied.
CostNo individual drops — the sky stays clean, but the clay charm thins out.
Round 4 · B

Clay stipple

Rain is a field of tiny clay beads: count = probability, size = mm; downpours switch to larger glossy beads with motion tails that settle low. Drizzle is a mist of specks, a front a dense wall, a shower a sparse pinch.

GoodThe most tactile, most clay-faithful rain; intensity is granular and unmistakable.
CostA front is implied by density, not drawn as one object; the verdict thins to dotted slivers.
Round 4 · C

Sheets & clusters

Rain is classified into weather systems: runs of 3+ wet hours are fronts — a curtain of near-vertical strokes thickening with mm, blurred underlay when heavy — while 1–2 hour runs are showers, three short slanted dashes that only occupy their hours.

GoodThe meteorological story — showers punctuate, fronts persist — which is exactly how you plan around weather.
CostPer-hour probability goes coarse; opacity is the carrier.
Round 4 · D

Wetness trace

One cumulative teal line per day: rain accumulates along a trace rising from the horizon (4mm fills the climb). Slope = intensity, a step = a shower, a long climb = a front, flat = dry — and three days become comparable at a glance.

GoodThe cleanest sky of the four; the cross-day comparison no other variant gives.
CostInstantaneous probability is not drawn at all — it lives in hover only.
Round 5 · the critique, built

Events, not hours — and drag the day

The critical read of Round 4 changed the atom: the event instant, not the hour cell. Sunrise and sunset sit as ticks at their exact minutes; their windows are tinted by the app's own event grade — green clouds above to catch the colour, amber mixed cloud, red overcast — and a bricked horizon (low cloud where the sun must appear) draws a ghost ticket: the event happens, but it fails. The moon stops being a flat rail: its altitude curve climbs through the night, and a ticket exists only where the curve passes the app's own 1–8° band behind the tower, one illumination gate shared with the plan. Rain is one quiet teal ground. And the omission this round exists for: drag any day — the strip is the transport. The preview above the rows is the scene: scrub through Tomorrow and watch the soup swallow the sun at exactly the hour the forecast says it dies. The hour-cell verdict strips are gone — the tickets are the verdict.

GoodEvery mark is a real event at a real minute, graded once by the same authority as the plan; dragging makes the forecast tangible.
CostThree tickets and a curve carry the story — the quiet days look almost empty, by design.
Round 6 · the brand pass, condensed

A clay skyline that keeps the appointments

Panel-sized: vertical sky at the scrubbed minute, three clay cloud decks, real rain, and the skyline of the eight photo-top-10 landmarks the app models — sprite-shot from their real clay meshes — each lighting up only inside its own alignment windows on a clear view. Scrub into them.

My shortlist

2 (Sky wash) if the strip should feel like weather — it scales from clear to soup gracefully and matches the scene. 5 (Verdict ribbon) if it should be a planner first — it is the fastest read in the set. My actual pick is a hybrid: the sky wash as the band, with the verdict grade carried by the existing tick heights and the cards, so colour never has to state a verdict twice.

What the data needs either way. The forecast fetch (cloudsFor in src/miniature/weather.js) currently asks Open-Meteo for cloud decks, wind and visibility only. Rain in any sketch means adding precipitation_probability and precipitation to the hourly request — same call, no new endpoint. The three days here are plausible November Berlin, not the live forecast; the winner gets wired to the real fetch.
Round 7 · Crazy 8 — the lens pass

What the pixel-art volume slider teaches

Two Opus dissections of the "volume slider filled with pixel art scenes": the track is the scale (a room that literally goes from day to night as you move along it), the thumb is the preview (a glass lens that magnifies and lights the scene beneath it), and there is no other chrome — control, value, preview and delight collapse into one object. Fused with the Round 6 critiques (data honesty, tap-to-commit, gesture ownership, a computed peak minute, the washout dead-end): eight directions, safest to wildest. The honest core in every one: one landmark, one computed alignment minute — sun azimuth crossing the tower's bearing — and a lens thumb that shows the disc actually touching the tower there. In-app, "commit" flies the 3D scene; here it says so.

1 · Lens on the band fixes: eclipse never depicted · dead thumb

Round 6's band keeps its job as transport, but the playhead becomes a glass lens: inside it, the disc pans with the sun's azimuth and drops with its altitude, touching the tower exactly at the computed minute — which the drag snaps to magnetically. Saturday shown; drag it.

2 · Eclipse tick, not a 40% peak fixes: fabricated peak

The same window twice-teaches: the dashed zone is Round 6's "peak = middle 40%" (up to ~9 minutes off); the orange hairline is the computed instant. The lens snaps to the hairline — contact is a moment, not a band. Drag inside the window.

3 · The scene eats the transport fixes: biggest surface owns no gesture · tap-to-commit

The middle band dissolves: the preview strip itself is the scrubber — drag anywhere on the scene — and the shootable windows sit pinned to its bottom edge as real buttons that jump to their minute and commit. (Skyline highlights still use Round 6's mock windows; the buttons are the honest one-landmark set.)

4 · Drench the dead days fixes: washout says nothing

Weather as content, not absence: rain renders as bead density over each day's light, and the verdict is spoken — a washed-out day says "soup" in words instead of showing an empty frame. The panel's first line answers "when do I go" even when the answer is no.

5 · Three lanes, one lens fixes: three stapled widgets · active-day opacity

The three day rows compress to three thin light lanes sharing one axis and one lens; the lens vignette always shows the best day at the scrubbed minute, and day comparison survives as window glows per lane — no opacity dimming, no separate preview panel.

6 · 72 hours, night as the seam fixes: three widgets · which-day flattening

One continuous track through three day/night cycles: midnight visibly resets the sky and relights it, so day boundaries need no chrome — the seam IS the night. Windows glow at their true minutes across all 72 hours; the lens scrubs the whole span.

7 · Windows are the only controls fixes: which-day at a glance · window = button

The image's trick at window scale: each shootable window becomes its own pill-slider with a scene inside — the disc approaches the tower as you scrub and touches at the alignment minute. Pill width encodes window length, so the three days compare as three objects. Tomorrow has nothing: its honest UI is a grey slug that says so.

8 · The whole panel is one eclipse pill wildest: everything is content

The image, scaled to our job: one pill holding 72 hours of Berlin light — three dawns, three dusks, the skyline as silhouette — with a single glass lens as the only control. Drag to scrub three sunsets; the lens vignette shows the composition at every instant; contact commits. (Accessibility keeps a text list of real windows alongside.)

Round 8 · the assembly round

One day at a time; the day is its shot-cards

The decisions from the last discussion, assembled from what Round 7 proved: one day is the work surface (one preview, tabs to change it), the day's shootable moments are a row of cards — each card a live mini-preview that scrubs inside its own window (drag) and commits on click, sun and moon as siblings in time order, no separate sliders. A day without light is one grey card that says so. Below it the 72h rail: orientation stays three days, verdicts in words, tap a lane to jump. Cards only exist where the maths exists — today that is the Fernsehturm; the Siegessäule plumbing (landmarks.ts already carries her profile) casts the next cards.

Round 9 · the mechanism the slider actually teaches

The throw — thumb stationary, the world moves

Every round so far moved a thumb; the volume slider does the opposite — you grab the world, throw it, and it coasts under a stationary needle, catching into detents. So: no lens-thumb, no cards to aim at. Grab the 72h strip anywhere and throw it. It carries real momentum, then settles into the nearest detent — every sunrise, sunset, midnight seam and eclipse minute is a detent. Land on the eclipse and the panel doesn't just depict contact, it performs it: the frame ignites and the shutter badge fires. Dead hours wear film perforations; the light is simply the strip between them. The needle is the only thing that never moves — where you look is always the centre. And the throw now carries a payload: catch an eclipse detent and the ticket unfolds beneath — the true frame at that focal length (tower filling, sun a 0.5° rim behind the shaft, none of the disc-the-size-of-the- sphere lie) plus stand · face · lens, ready to commit.
